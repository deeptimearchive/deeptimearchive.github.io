A ship is drifting in the Pacific near Fiji, listing heavily. She is still afloat. But she is empty. There is no one aboard. Her chronometer is gone. Her log book is gone. So are a couple of firearms. In the medical bag there are bloody bandages. Twenty-five people sailed on this ship. Where did they go?

A luxury yacht with a second life

Her name was the Joyita. She was built in 1931 as a luxury yacht for the film director Roland West. Years later her story took a very different turn: Katharine Luomala lent the Joyita to a friend, Captain Thomas Miller.

On 3 October 1955 the Joyita left Apia, in Samoa, bound for Tokelau with twenty-five people aboard. Sixteen were crew. Nine were passengers - among them a doctor, a government official and two children. The voyage was expected to take about two days.

One more detail about the ship: cork lining had been added to her hull, along with refrigeration equipment. Hold on to that. It matters later.

Two days became thirty-seven

The Joyita never arrived. Search planes went looking for her and covered more than 260,000 square kilometres of the Pacific - a staggering stretch of ocean. They found nothing.

Then, thirty-seven days after she left Apia, the Joyita was found, drifting and listing north of the Fijian island of Vanua Levu. Captain Gerald Douglas of the ship Tuvalu came across her. There was no one aboard. Not the crew. Not the passengers. Not the children.

The chronometer, the log book and a couple of firearms were missing. In the medical bag, there were bloody bandages.

A ship that could barely sink

Here is what makes the story so strange. Her extreme buoyancy - remember the cork lining - made sinking nearly impossible. So twenty-five people had left a ship that was in no danger of going down. Why would anyone do that?

"Inexplicable"

An official inquiry examined the case. Its conclusion was remarkably honest: the fate of the passengers and crew was inexplicable on the evidence submitted.

There is a theory. Auckland academic David Wright believes the boat had been taking on water from a corroded pipe in the engine cooling system. It is a theory, not a proven answer - and even a leak would not explain everything. Why were the chronometer and the log book missing? Why the firearms? And whose blood was on the bandages?

Not a ghost story

That is what sets the Joyita apart from so many sea legends. She was a real ship with a real passenger list, found in real waters, and an official inquiry called her fate inexplicable. This is not a ghost story. It is a documented case - with names, dates and evidence - and still no answer.

The sea gave back the ship. It kept the answer.
